Australia's Black Summer
Fire, Forest & Wildlife

The 2019–20 bushfire season was the most catastrophic in Australia's recorded history. This visualisation explores the scale, geography, and ecological impact of the fires that burned for months across the continent.

01 — Where Did It Burn?

A Nation on Fire — Geographic Scope of Black Summer

No Australian state or territory was untouched. The Northern Territory recorded the largest total burned area (12.6 Mha), but the most dramatic departure from normal was in New South Wales and Victoria — typically mild fire states — where forests burned on an unprecedented scale. Hover over each state to see its total.

Source: ABARES, Fires in Australia's Forests 2016–21 (2024). Area in millions of hectares (Mha) includes forest and non-forest land burned by all fire types. Map uses ABS State & Territory boundaries, STE 2016.

The Northern Territory dominates by total area due to its vast size and high fire frequency from grassland and savanna burning. What makes Black Summer exceptional is that NSW (+1,779% above its four-season average) and Victoria (+1,132%) burned at levels never recorded before.
02 — How Big Was It Really?

Five Seasons of Fire — Putting Black Summer in Context

To understand how extreme the 2019–20 season was, we need to compare it against previous years. Both total area and the split between planned (prescribed) burns and unplanned (wildfire) burning tell an important story.

Black Summer (2019–20) burned 27.3 Mha across Australia's forests and surrounding land — the second-highest total in this five-year window, but devastating for the heavily populated south-east.

Click the legend to highlight Planned or Unplanned fire lines. 2019–20 saw the lowest planned burn total in five years — a factor that may have contributed to severity. Source: ABARES 2024.

🔥 Why does 2017–18 show the highest total? The Northern Territory experienced exceptional savanna fire that season (29.8 Mha), inflating the national total. In contrast, Black Summer's destruction was concentrated in the densely forested, populated south-east — making its human and ecological impact far more severe per hectare.
03 — Planned vs. Unplanned Burning

The Role of Fire Management — Did Planned Burns Help?

Prescribed (planned) burning is a key fire management tool in Australia. Governments and fire agencies use it to reduce fuel loads before the dangerous summer fire season. Examining the ratio of planned to unplanned fire reveals whether this management kept pace.

Each bar represents 100% of fire area for that season, split by fire type. In 2019–20, unplanned fires accounted for 76% of all burned area — and the absolute planned burn total was the lowest across all five seasons at just 6.5 Mha. Source: ABARES 2024.

Key Finding

The 2019–20 season had the lowest planned burn area of any season studied. Drought conditions during 2019 made prescribed burning difficult or impossible in many regions, removing a critical buffer against the fires that followed.

05 — What Burned?

Vegetation Types and the Scale of Destruction

The fire's reach extended across multiple vegetation types, but native forest — which is home to Australia's most unique wildlife — bore the brunt of the unplanned burning. The connected dot chart reveals how dramatically Black Summer exceeded normal fire seasons for densely populated states.

Native forest accounted for 20.9 Mha of the 2019–20 fire area — the vast majority unplanned (wildfire). Commercial plantations and other forests were also affected. Source: ABARES 2024.

Each line connects Black Summer (red dot) to the average of the other four seasons (teal dot). For NSW, Black Summer was 23× above the seasonal average; for Victoria, 12×. Source: ABARES 2024.

06 — The Ecological Toll

331 Threatened Species in the Path of the Fires

Beyond the land itself, the fires tore through the habitat of hundreds of species already listed as threatened under Australia's Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act. The analysis below is based on spatial overlap between fire extent and modelled species distributions.

Of the 331 EPBC-listed threatened species in fire-affected zones, 31 were Critically Endangered — meaning a significant portion of each species' entire known distribution was within the burn scar. For some species, the fires may have been an extinction-level event.

56% Vulnerable, 33% Endangered, 9% Critically Endangered. Source: DCCEEW, January 2020.

Plants dominate by count (272 species), but frogs, mammals and reptiles carry disproportionate ecological weight — many are endemic to south-east Australia. Source: DCCEEW, January 2020.

Bubble size represents number of species. Each bubble shows where a group sits in the threat spectrum. Frogs and birds punch above their weight — a disproportionate share of frog species are Critically Endangered. Click a threat level in the legend to isolate it. Source: DCCEEW, January 2020.

Conclusion

An Unprecedented Season — and a Warning

Australia has always had bushfires. But Black Summer was different: it was larger, longer, and more geographically spread than any season on record. States like NSW and Victoria — which had historically low fire activity — experienced fire on a scale that exceeded their entire four-year average by more than an order of magnitude.

The ecological cost was immediate and severe. Over 300 threatened species had their habitat overlap with burn scars. Recovery for many will take decades; for some, the Black Summer may mark a permanent decline.

The fire management data reveals a troubling pattern: planned burn area was at its lowest precisely when unplanned fire was at its most destructive. Extended drought conditions in 2019 created a perfect storm — record fuel loads, record temperatures, and record fire behaviour.

Climate projections indicate that conditions similar to or worse than 2019 will become increasingly common. Understanding the scale of Black Summer is not only a historical exercise — it is essential preparation for what may come.
