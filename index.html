Australia's Black Summer
Fire, Forest & Wildlife

The 2019–20 bushfire season was the most catastrophic in Australia's recorded history. This visualisation explores the scale, geography, and ecological impact of the fires that burned for months across the continent.

27.3M Hectares burned
331 Threatened species affected
8 States & territories
5 Seasons analysed
01 — Where Did It Burn?

A Nation on Fire — Geographic Scope of Black Summer

No Australian state or territory was untouched. Areas on this map are measured in Mha (million hectares) — one Mha equals ten thousand square kilometres, roughly the size of greater Sydney. The Northern Territory recorded the largest total burned area (12.6 Mha), but the most dramatic departure from normal was in New South Wales and Victoria, where forests burned on an unprecedented scale. Hover over each state to see its total.

Total area includes forest and non-forest land burned by all fire types.

The Northern Territory dominates by total area due to its vast size and high frequency of savanna burning. What makes Black Summer exceptional is that NSW — normally a low-fire state — burned 23 times above its seasonal average, and Victoria burned 12 times above its average.
02 — How Big Was It Really?

Five Seasons of Fire — Putting Black Summer in Context

To understand how extreme 2019–20 was, we compare it against the four preceding seasons. Both total area burned and the split between planned (prescribed) burns and unplanned wildfires tell an important story. Click the legend in either chart to focus on a fire type.

Black Summer (2019–20) burned 27.3 million hectares — the second-highest total in this five-year window, but devastating for the heavily populated south-east.

The red dashed line marks the Black Summer unplanned peak. In 2019–20, the planned burn total was the lowest of any season — drought conditions made prescribed burning unsafe across large areas.

Why does 2017–18 show the highest national total? The Northern Territory experienced exceptional savanna fires that season (29.8 Mha), inflating the national figure. Black Summer's destruction was concentrated in the densely forested, populated south-east — making its human and ecological impact far more severe per hectare.
03 — Planned vs. Unplanned Burning

Fire Management Under Pressure — Who Burned What, and How?

Prescribed (planned) burning is Australia's primary tool for reducing dangerous fuel loads before fire season. The two charts below show the national balance of planned vs. unplanned fire across all five seasons, and how that breakdown played out differently in each state during Black Summer.

Each bar represents 100% of burned area for that season. In 2019–20, unplanned wildfires made up 76% of all fire area — while the planned burn total was the lowest of any season at just 6.5 million hectares.

Stacked bars show planned (teal) vs. unplanned (orange) burning by state in 2019–20. Labels show each state's total. Nearly all NSW and Victorian burning was unplanned wildfire.

04 — State Rankings Over Time

How Fire Burden Shifted Across States — Five Seasons

These two views reveal the same story at different scales: the bump chart tracks each state's rank position across five seasons, while the heatmap shows the absolute intensity. The 2019–20 column tells the Black Summer story at a glance. Click the legend on the bump chart to follow individual states.

State rankings by total fire area (1 = most burned). NSW rose sharply from 4th to 3rd in Black Summer; Victoria jumped to 4th, overtaking Western Australia.

Cell values show Mha burned (cells under 0.5 Mha are left blank for readability). The sudden darkening of NSW and Victoria in 2019–20 stands out sharply against their pale cells in earlier seasons.

05 — What Burned?

Vegetation Types and the True Scale of Destruction

The fire swept across all vegetation categories, but native forest — home to Australia's most unique wildlife — bore the overwhelming share of unplanned burning. The comparison chart on the right shows just how far outside the historical range Black Summer was for the states that matter most.

Lollipop chart showing total burned area by vegetation type in 2019–20. Native forest (20.9 Mha) dwarfs every other category — more than three times the area of all non-forest land burned.

Each line connects a state's Black Summer total (red) to its average across the other four seasons (teal). For NSW, the gap represents a 23× increase; for Victoria, 12×. Queensland and Tasmania burned less than their averages.

06 — The Ecological Toll

331 Threatened Species in the Path of the Fires

Beyond the land itself, the fires swept through the habitat of hundreds of species already listed as threatened under Australia's Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act — the national law governing threatened wildlife. The analysis below is based on spatial overlap between fire extent and modelled species distributions.

Of the 331 EPBC-listed threatened species in fire-affected zones, 31 were Critically Endangered — the highest threat category, meaning a significant portion of each species' entire known range was within the burn scar. For some species, the Black Summer may mark a permanent population decline.

56% Vulnerable  |  33% Endangered  |  9% Critically Endangered.

Grouped bars show each threat level side by side within each species group. Plants dominate by count (272 species), but frogs and reptiles are disproportionately represented among Critically Endangered species.

Bubble size encodes the number of species in each group–status combination. Frogs punch far above their weight: 3 of 14 frog species in fire zones are Critically Endangered.

Conclusion

An Unprecedented Season — and a Warning

Australia has always had bushfires. But Black Summer was different: larger, longer, and more geographically devastating than any season on record. States like NSW and Victoria — which had historically low fire activity — experienced fire on a scale that exceeded their entire four-year average by more than an order of magnitude.

The ecological cost was immediate and severe. Over 330 threatened species had their habitat overlap with burn scars. Recovery for many will take decades; for some, the Black Summer may mark a permanent decline toward extinction.

The fire management data reveals a troubling pattern: planned burn area was at its lowest precisely when unplanned wildfire was at its most destructive. Extended drought conditions in 2019 created a perfect storm — record fuel loads, record temperatures, and record fire behaviour across the south-east.

Climate projections indicate that conditions similar to or worse than 2019 will become increasingly common. Understanding the full scale of Black Summer is not only a historical exercise — it is essential preparation for what may come next.
